\begin{abstract}
Accurate risk stratification may facilitate timely referral for sleep assessment, yet models developed from small tabular datasets are particularly susceptible to outcome ambiguity, preprocessing leakage, and optimistic validation. This study develops an explainable stacking framework using the publicly available Sleep Health and Lifestyle Dataset, comprising 374 participant-level observations, 11 mixed-type predictors, and three outcome categories: no recorded disorder, insomnia, and sleep apnea. Administrative identifiers were excluded, blood pressure was decomposed into systolic and diastolic components, and every data-dependent transformation was estimated exclusively from the training partition. Random Forest and XGBoost base learners were combined through a logistic-regression meta-learner fitted to out-of-fold class probabilities. Model behaviour was examined using complementary SHAP and LIME analyses, while discrimination was assessed on a stratified 20\% hold-out set. The proposed stack correctly classified 73 of 75 observations, corresponding to an accuracy of 97.33\% and a macro-F1 score of 0.96. Class-specific F1 scores were 0.93 for insomnia, 1.00 for no recorded disorder, and 0.94 for sleep apnea. Paired bootstrap analysis estimated a 1.33-percentage-point improvement over RF--XGBoost soft voting, although the confidence interval included zero and the exact McNemar test was non-significant. These findings establish a reproducible computational benchmark rather than a clinically deployable diagnostic system. Independent outcome adjudication, repeated internal validation, calibration analysis, and prospective external evaluation remain necessary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sleep disorders impose a substantial burden through impaired daytime function, reduced quality of life, and increased neurocognitive and cardiometabolic risk. Obstructive sleep apnea is of particular concern because clinically important disease may remain unrecognised until complications emerge \cite{aasm2023,osareview2024,sleepai2023}. Although polysomnography remains central to definitive assessment, its cost, infrastructure requirements, and limited availability constrain population-scale use. Data-driven risk stratification could therefore support earlier referral by identifying individuals who warrant comprehensive clinical evaluation \cite{aasm2023,osareview2024}.

Structured lifestyle and physiological data offer a pragmatic basis for developing such tools, but they also present methodological challenges. Predictors differ in scale and representation, categorical levels require harmonisation, and clinically relevant classes may be unequally represented. Moreover, performance can be materially inflated when preprocessing is conducted before data partitioning, hyperparameters are implicitly adapted to the test set, or uncertainty from a single split is ignored \cite{stats2024,kapoor2023}. Reliable evaluation consequently requires strict separation of development and test data, class-sensitive metrics, paired uncertainty analysis, and transparent limits on clinical interpretation.

This study addresses these requirements through four contributions. First, it specifies an auditable preprocessing strategy for mixed numerical and categorical variables, with all learned transformations confined to the training partition. Second, it develops a heterogeneous stack in which Random Forest and XGBoost contribute complementary class-probability estimates to a logistic meta-learner \cite{tabular2023,xgbreview2023,ensemble2023}. Third, it evaluates the proposed model against soft voting and previously reported ANN ensembles, including paired uncertainty analysis for the two models tested on identical observations. Fourth, it combines SHAP and LIME to examine global importance and local attribution patterns while explicitly separating predictive association from causal or diagnostic interpretation \cite{xaisurvey2023,explain2024}.

\section{Related work and study rationale}
Machine-learning models for sleep assessment should be positioned as decision-support instruments rather than substitutes for clinical diagnosis. Their appropriate role is to prioritise further evaluation, not to replace medical history, physical examination, polysomnography, or specialist judgement \cite{aasm2023}. This distinction is especially important here because formal sleep-disorder taxonomies are considerably more granular than the three outcome categories represented in the study dataset.

For structured data, tree-based ensembles remain competitive because they accommodate nonlinear effects, interactions, and mixed predictive scales with limited distributional assumptions \cite{tabular2023}. Random Forest reduces variance through aggregation of decorrelated trees, whereas XGBoost sequentially regularises residual error through gradient boosting \cite{xgbreview2023}. Their distinct inductive biases motivate stacking: a second-level learner can integrate out-of-fold probability estimates and exploit complementary error structures that are inaccessible to either model alone \cite{ensemble2023,stackreview3}. The resulting predictive gains must nevertheless be evaluated within a leakage-controlled pipeline and accompanied by explanation methods that expose, rather than obscure, the model's dependence on individual variables \cite{clinicalreport2024,xaisurvey2023,explain2024}.

\section{Materials and methods}
\subsection{Methodological overview}
The investigation followed a prespecified, leakage-controlled analytical pathway (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}). Data integrity and outcome coding were examined before modelling, after which the administrative identifier was removed and clinically interpretable features were derived. A stratified test partition was then isolated prior to fitting any transformation. Within the development partition, numerical variables were standardised, categorical variables were one-hot encoded, and out-of-fold probability estimates were generated for both base learners. These estimates formed the input to a logistic-regression meta-learner. Final discrimination, paired model comparisons, and explanation analyses were conducted only after the complete pipeline had been fixed. This design preserves the independence of the test set and prevents information from the evaluation sample from influencing representation learning or model construction.

\subsection{Data source and outcome definition}
The analysis used the publicly available Sleep Health and Lifestyle Dataset hosted on Kaggle \cite{kaggle2023}. The dataset comprises 374 participant-level observations and 13 variables: one administrative identifier, 11 candidate predictors, and the Sleep Disorder outcome. The predictors represent demographic characteristics, sleep behaviour, physical activity, perceived stress, body-mass category, heart rate, and blood pressure. Because the data were not collected as a prospectively recruited clinical cohort and the outcome was not independently adjudicated, the dataset is treated as a methodological benchmark for risk stratification rather than as a source of prevalence estimates or diagnostic ground truth.

All 11 predictors were complete, whereas 219 outcome entries were blank. Consistent with the coding used in the analytical dataset, blank outcomes were assigned to the no-recorded-disorder category, producing 219 observations with no recorded disorder, 77 with insomnia, and 78 with sleep apnea. This operational definition is consequential because absence of a recorded label is not equivalent to clinician-confirmed absence of disease. The assignment was therefore retained for reproducibility but treated as a primary source of outcome uncertainty throughout interpretation.

Age ranged from 27 to 59 years (mean 42.18, standard deviation 8.67), and reported sleep duration ranged from 5.8 to 8.5 h (mean 7.13, standard deviation 0.80). Quality-of-sleep scores ranged from 4 to 9, physical-activity levels from 30 to 90, stress scores from 3 to 8, heart rate from 65 to 86 beats/min, and daily step counts from 3,000 to 10,000. The categorical variables comprised two gender categories, 11 occupational groups, and four body-mass-index labels. Blood pressure, originally represented as a systolic/diastolic string, was separated into two numerical variables. The synonymous labels ``Normal Weight'' and ``Normal'' were consolidated before one-hot encoding.

Person ID was excluded because it contains ordering information without a plausible physiological interpretation. Occupation was retained as a contextual predictor, although its contribution requires careful scrutiny because it may encode socioeconomic structure and introduce transportability concerns. The public-source provenance, modest sample size, outcome-assignment rule, and absence of clinical adjudication therefore define the inferential boundaries of this study.

\subsection{Data splitting and leakage control}
An 80:20 stratified partition with random seed 42 produced a development set of 299 observations and an independent test set of 75 observations. The split was performed before numerical scaling, categorical encoding, or model fitting. All preprocessing parameters---including numerical means and variances, categorical vocabularies, and label mappings---were estimated from the development data and subsequently applied without modification to the test set. The same principle was maintained within cross-validation folds used to construct the stack. This ordering prevents evaluation-set information from influencing either the feature representation or the fitted decision function \cite{stats2024,kapoor2023}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Leakage-safe data preparation}
\label{alg:prep}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Dataset $D$, target $y$, seed $s=42$
\Ensure Transformed partitions $(X'_{tr},y_{tr})$ and $(X'_{te},y_{te})$
\State Remove identifier fields and assign blank outcomes to no recorded disorder
\State Harmonise BMI labels and split Blood Pressure into two integers
\State Perform an 80:20 stratified split using seed $s$
\State Fit the scaler and one-hot encoder on $X_{tr}$ only
\State Transform $X_{tr}$ and $X_{te}$ using the fitted objects
\State Fit the label map on $y_{tr}$ and apply it to $y_{te}$
\State \Return $(X'_{tr},y_{tr})$, $(X'_{te},y_{te})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Base learners and stacking design}
The proposed architecture combines two tree-based learners with complementary bias--variance characteristics. The Random Forest comprised 100 trees with a maximum depth of 10, a minimum split size of 5, a minimum leaf size of 2, and random seed 42. XGBoost used 800 boosting iterations, a learning rate of 0.02, maximum depth 3, row and column subsampling rates of 0.90, minimum child weight 3, $\gamma=0.15$, and $\lambda=1.2$. For each observation in the development set, five-fold cross-validation generated out-of-fold class probabilities from both learners. Their concatenation formed the feature space for a multinomial logistic-regression meta-learner. The base models were subsequently refitted on the full development set for inference on unseen observations. Restricting meta-learner training to out-of-fold predictions is essential; using in-sample probabilities would expose the second level to optimistically biased representations \cite{ensemble2023,stackreview3}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Out-of-fold stacking prediction}
\label{alg:stack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $(X'_{tr},y_{tr})$, test vector $x$, $K=5$ folds
\Ensure Predicted class $\hat y$
\For{$k=1$ to $K$}
\State Fit $f_{RF}^{(-k)}$ and $f_{XGB}^{(-k)}$ outside fold $k$
\State Store probabilities on fold $k$ as $P_{RF}^{(k)}$ and $P_{XGB}^{(k)}$
\EndFor
\State Concatenate out-of-fold probabilities $P=[P_{RF},P_{XGB}]$
\State Fit logistic meta-learner $g$ on $(P,y_{tr})$
\State Fit both base learners on all $X'_{tr}$
\State $\hat y\gets\arg\max_c g([f_{RF}(x),f_{XGB}(x)])_c$
\State \Return $\hat y$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Explainability and auditability}
SHAP explanations were obtained through a permutation-based, model-agnostic interface. A fixed background sample was drawn exclusively from the development partition, and each perturbed observation was passed through the complete preprocessing and prediction pipeline. For feature $j$, global SHAP importance was defined as
\begin{equation}
I_j=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\phi_{ij}|,
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{ij}$ denotes the attribution assigned to feature $j$ for observation $i$. LIME was used as a complementary local analysis: neighbourhoods were generated around individual test observations, the ensemble was queried on the perturbed samples, and an interpretable weighted linear surrogate approximated the local decision surface. Absolute LIME coefficients were aggregated only to assess broad agreement with SHAP rankings; they were not treated as a second estimate of causal importance. Both methods characterise the fitted prediction function, and correlated predictors may share or exchange attribution \cite{xaisurvey2023,explain2024,dependence2023}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Permutation-SHAP model audit}
\label{alg:shap}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fitted model $F$, training matrix $X_{tr}$, explanation sample $X_e$
\Ensure Ranked importance vector $I$
\State Sample a fixed background $B\subset X_{tr}$
\State Define $h(z)=F(\operatorname{decode}(z))$
\State Initialise a permutation explainer with $h$ and $B$
\State Compute $\Phi$ for $X_e$
\State $I_j\gets\operatorname{mean}_i(|\Phi_{ij}|)$ for each feature $j$
\State Rank features by decreasing $I_j$
\State \Return $I$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental protocol}
The prespecified evaluation used the isolated 75-observation test partition. Accuracy was reported alongside class-specific precision, recall, and F1 score, as well as macro- and support-weighted summaries. For class $c$, precision and recall were defined as $TP_c/(TP_c+FP_c)$ and $TP_c/(TP_c+FN_c)$, respectively, with F1 calculated as their harmonic mean. Macro-F1 was emphasised because it assigns equal weight to each outcome category despite unequal class frequencies. The proposed stack and soft-voting comparator were additionally evaluated through 20,000 paired bootstrap resamples of test observations and an exact McNemar test based on discordant predictions. The analysis assesses discrimination within the available benchmark; it does not substitute for repeated internal validation, probability calibration, subgroup analysis, or evaluation in an untouched external cohort \cite{stats2024,steyerberg2024,collins2024}.



\section{Results}
The proposed stacking model correctly classified 73 of 75 test observations, yielding an accuracy of 97.33\% and a macro-F1 score of 0.96. It identified 13 of 15 insomnia observations, all 44 observations with no recorded disorder, and all 16 sleep-apnea observations. Both errors involved insomnia being classified as sleep apnea; no observation was incorrectly assigned to the no-recorded-disorder category. The RF--XGBoost soft-voting comparator achieved 96.00\% accuracy and a macro-F1 score of 0.94 on the same test set.

\subsection{Paired statistical comparison}
Because stacking and soft voting were evaluated on identical test observations, their predictions permitted a paired comparison. The 20,000-resample bootstrap estimated a stacking-minus-soft-voting accuracy difference of 1.33 percentage points, with a 95\% percentile interval of $-2.67$ to $5.33$ percentage points. The corresponding discordance table was $\begin{bmatrix}71&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$, and the exact McNemar test yielded $p=1.000$. Thus, the observed numerical advantage was not statistically distinguishable from chance variation in this small test sample.

\section{Discussion}
The proposed heterogeneous stack achieved the strongest discrimination among the evaluated configurations, suggesting that the Random Forest and XGBoost probability estimates contain complementary information. The logistic meta-learner improved accuracy by 1.33 percentage points relative to soft voting; however, the paired confidence interval crossed zero and the McNemar test was non-significant. The principal finding is therefore improved point performance within this benchmark, not established statistical superiority.

Error patterns provide additional context beyond aggregate accuracy. Recall was perfect for the no-recorded-disorder and sleep-apnea categories, whereas two insomnia observations were assigned to sleep apnea. This asymmetry may reflect overlap in the available lifestyle and physiological features, but the sample is too small to determine whether it represents a stable clinical phenotype or split-specific variation. In a decision-support setting, such errors would have different consequences from missed apnea; operating thresholds should therefore be selected using clinically defined utilities rather than accuracy alone \cite{decisioncurve2023,steyerberg2024}.

The architecture is methodologically plausible because bagging and boosting impose different inductive biases. Random Forest stabilises high-variance trees through aggregation, whereas XGBoost sequentially concentrates on residual error. Stacking can learn when each probability profile is informative, provided that the meta-learner is trained on out-of-fold predictions and every transformation remains within the development pipeline \cite{ensemble2023,stats2024}. These safeguards are central to the interpretation of the reported performance.

SHAP and LIME add complementary views of model behaviour, but neither resolves causal validity, fairness, or calibration. Attribution rankings may change when predictors are correlated, and agreement between explanation methods does not establish a medical mechanism \cite{rudin2023,dependence2023}. Before any translational use, the model would require probability calibration, decision-curve analysis, subgroup-specific error assessment, robustness to missingness and measurement shift, and evaluation of how clinicians interpret its outputs \cite{clinicalxai2024,explain2024}.

\section{Limitations}
Several limitations constrain generalisation. First, the sample is modest and its recruitment mechanism is insufficiently documented, limiting assessment of selection and spectrum bias. Second, blank outcomes were assigned to no recorded disorder; without clinical adjudication, this rule may introduce outcome misclassification. Third, performance was estimated from a single hold-out partition, and the paired analysis consequently has limited power. Fourth, no independent cohort, temporal validation set, or prospective assessment was available. Fifth, the outcomes do not encode polysomnographic severity, treatment status, comorbidity burden, or longitudinal change. Finally, the explanation analysis is sensitive to the selected background distribution and to dependence among predictors. These limitations preclude claims of diagnostic validity or clinical readiness.

\section{Conclusion}
This study presents a leakage-controlled and explainable RF--XGBoost stacking framework for multiclass sleep-disorder risk stratification. On the isolated test set, the model achieved 97.33\% accuracy and a macro-F1 score of 0.96, with a modest but statistically inconclusive improvement over soft voting. The analysis demonstrates the computational potential of heterogeneous stacking while exposing the uncertainty introduced by limited sample size and non-adjudicated outcomes. Future work should prioritise repeated internal validation, calibration and clinical-utility analysis, subgroup robustness, and prospective evaluation against independently adjudicated outcomes before the framework is considered for decision-support use.
